How to read your estimated 1RM chart

A one-rep max, or 1RM, is the most you could lift once. You almost never test it, so a workout tracker estimates it from the sets you actually do. That estimate turns every session into one number per exercise, and the estimated 1RM chart plots it over months. This guide explains where the number comes from, why it moves the way it does, how to use it to pick your next weight, and when to ignore it.

Last updated 21 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • Your estimated 1RM comes from the best set of each workout: weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30), the Epley formula.
  • It rises when you add weight or reps at the same weight. It is capped at 12 reps, so high-rep sets don't move it.
  • Read the trend over months, not the dots week to week. A dip after a break is normal.
  • Use it to choose weights (10 reps ≈ 75% of your 1RM), never as a reason to test a heavy single.

What an estimated 1RM is

Your true one-rep max changes with sleep, warm-up and nerves, and testing it is risky and tiring. An estimated 1RM is a formula applied to a normal set: if you lifted 80 kg for 10 clean reps, how much could you probably lift once? Several formulas exist; IronBuddy uses Epley, the most common one: weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30).

80 kg × (1 + 10 ÷ 30) = 80 × 1.333 = 106.7 kg. Do 11 reps next week and it becomes 109.3 kg; add 2.5 kg and get 10 again, and it is 110 kg. Either kind of progress moves the same number, which is the whole point: it lets you compare a 6-rep day with a 10-rep day on one line.

How IronBuddy calculates it

  • Best set of the workout. Every set you logged with both a weight and reps is estimated, and the highest estimate is that session's point on the chart. The other sets don't pull it down.
  • Reps capped at 12. A set of 15 or 20 is counted as 12. Above about 12 reps the formula overestimates, and the set says more about endurance than about strength.
  • Rounded to 0.1 kg (or lb). Units follow your settings.
  • Per exercise. A barbell bench press and a machine chest press are two different lines. Their numbers are not comparable, and neither is comparable with a friend's.
  • Drop sets and rest-pause count. Each drop or pause chunk is a set with its own weight and reps, so a heavy first chunk can be the best set.

Reading the chart

Open Progress → Exercises, pick the exercise and tap 1RM. The headline is your latest estimate and how much it changed over the range you picked: “105 kg, ▲ +25 kg since you started”. Under it, one point per session.

  • Zoom out. On 1W or 1M the line is jagged, because every session is a different day. On 3M or 6M, IronBuddy groups sessions into weeks or months, and the shape is the truth: climbing, flat, or falling.
  • A plateau is a flat month, not a flat week. Two or three sessions at the same number is normal. Six weeks flat on a lift you train twice a week is a plateau; change something (reps, exercise order, a back-off set, more food).
  • Dips after breaks are expected. A holiday, an illness or a deload shows as a step down and a quick climb back. Don't read it as lost strength; read it as a reset.
  • Sudden jumps usually mean a form change. A shorter range of motion, a bounce, a new seat height. If a jump feels too good, check the set, not the chart.
  • Compare with Best weight. The Best weight chart only moves when you add load; the 1RM chart moves with reps too. If 1RM climbs while Best weight is flat, you are getting more reps and it is time to add weight.

Using it to pick your next weight

Because the formula links reps to a share of your max, you can turn it round: if your estimated 1RM on the leg press is 200 kg and the plan says 10 reps, start around 75% of that, 150 kg.

When the number misleads

  • High-rep sets. Anything over 12 reps is capped, and sets of 20 are not really about strength at all.
  • New exercises. The first few sessions on a lift improve fast because you are learning it, not because you got that much stronger.
  • Machines with odd stacks. Plate-loaded and selectorised machines differ between gyms; the same number means different things on different equipment.
  • Bodyweight exercises. Pull-ups and push-ups logged without added weight have no load to estimate from, so they have no 1RM line. Log the added weight if you use a belt or vest.
  • Testing it. An estimate is not a plan for a heavy single. If you want a true max, work up to it over weeks with a spotter, not because a chart said 110.

Estimated 1RM questions

Why did my 1RM drop when I lifted the same weight?

Because you got fewer reps with it. The estimate uses weight and reps together: 80 kg for 10 estimates 106.7 kg, 80 kg for 8 estimates 101.3 kg. Same bar, fewer reps, lower number. One bad day is noise; three sessions in a row is a trend worth a look at sleep, food and the sets before that exercise.

I did 15 reps and the chart didn't move. Why?

The formula is only reliable up to about 12 reps, so IronBuddy counts any set above 12 as 12. Past that point, reps say more about endurance than about your maximum. If you want the chart to move, add weight and work in the 6–12 range.

How accurate is an estimated 1RM?

Within roughly 5 to 10 percent for most people on most lifts, better at low reps and on exercises you do often, worse on high reps, new exercises and machines with odd weight stacks. Treat it as a trend line and a planning tool, not a promise. Never attempt a heavy single because a chart says you can.

A note on safety

This guide is general training information, not medical advice. An estimated 1RM is a trend number, not permission to attempt a maximum lift; choose loads that suit your experience, and speak to a qualified professional if you have an injury or a health condition.
